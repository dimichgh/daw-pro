## 1. Streaming loudness normalisation — current values

### Spotify — **[PLATFORM]**
- Target: **-14 LUFS integrated**, per the ITU-R BS.1770 loudness algorithm.
- Source: Spotify for Artists / Spotify Support, "Loudness normalization" — https://support.spotify.com/artists/article/loudness-normalization (current live page, no revision date published on the page itself; content consistent with Spotify's loudness policy since ~2017).
- On by default: yes, in the Spotify app. Spotify's own page explicitly carves out an exception: **the web player and third-party playback devices (TVs, some connected speakers) do not apply loudness normalization** — flag this on screen, it's the most commonly missed caveat.
- Turns quiet tracks up as well as down: **yes**. Spotify's own text: "Positive gain is applied to softer masters so the loudness level is -14 dB LUFS." This directly contradicts the common claim that Spotify (like YouTube) is "turn down only" — that claim is TRUE for YouTube but FALSE for Spotify. Correct this on screen; it is one of the most repeated errors in mastering content.
- True peak guidance: Spotify recommends **≤ -1 dBTP** generally, and **≤ -2 dBTP if your master is louder than -14 LUFS integrated** (extra headroom because loud masters are more exposed to transcoding overshoot). Same source page.
- Note: Spotify also has loudness *presets* users can pick (Loud / Normal / Quiet, historically documented around -11 / -14 / -19 LUFS in third-party mastering literature) — I could not find these preset numbers on Spotify's own current support page, so treat any -11/-19 figures as **[MEASURED/THIRD-PARTY, unverified against a live Spotify source as of 2026-09-03]** and do not put them on screen as Spotify's own words.

## 2. True peak vs. sample peak

- **Sample peak** only measures the level at the digital sample points. **True peak (dBTP)** estimates the actual reconstructed analog waveform level between samples after D/A (or, equivalently, after lossy decoding), because a brick-wall-limited digital signal can still overshoot between samples when reconstructed — these are **inter-sample peaks (ISPs)**.
- Why a -0.1 dBFS sample-peak file can clip after lossy encoding: lossy codecs (MP3, AAC, Opus, etc.) re-encode/decode the waveform using a different reconstruction filter and can shift the interpolated peak higher than the original PCM's sample peak; a file with headroom by sample-peak measurement alone can still overshoot 0 dBFS after the codec round-trip, causing audible clipping/distortion on the transcoded copy even though the source file "measured fine." **[general engineering fact, well established, no single canonical citation needed beyond ITU-R BS.1770]**
- **Oversampling requirement — [STANDARD]**: ITU-R BS.1770-4 (2015) specifies **4x oversampling** as the method for estimating true peak (interpolating 3 extra samples between each pair of real samples using a defined FIR/windowed-sinc filter, then taking the maximum absolute value of the oversampled signal). Source: Recommendation ITU-R BS.1770, current edition BS.1770-5 (Nov 2023), https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf — the true-peak algorithm and the 4x minimum oversampling requirement carried forward from BS.1770-4 (2015) into -5 (2023) unchanged; some commercial loudness meters oversample higher than 4x for extra margin, but 4x is the specified minimum.
- **Ceiling mastering engineers actually recommend — [MEASURED/THIRD-PARTY, industry consensus]**: **-1 dBTP** is the standard safe ceiling for streaming distribution (enough margin that a master usually survives AAC/MP3 transcoding without new clipping). Some engineers go more conservative at **-1.5 or -2 dBTP**, and Spotify's own support page explicitly recommends **-2 dBTP specifically when the integrated loudness is above -14 LUFS**, i.e., loud/limited masters need more true-peak headroom, not less, because they're more exposed to codec overshoot. A 0 dBFS-sample-peak master very often clips after transcoding; a -1 dBTP master usually does not.

## 3. Mono compatibility

- **Why bass is kept mono**: long wavelengths at low frequencies (a 100 Hz wave is ~3.4 m) mean small differences in left/right phase or arrival time — from stereo widening plugins, mic placement, or unison/detune effects — can cause **destructive interference (phase cancellation)** when the two channels are summed to mono. Content that is out-of-phase between L and R cancels partially or fully at the frequencies where the two signals are ~180° apart; for bass specifically, a fully out-of-phase low end can nearly disappear in mono, which is disproportionately damaging because bass carries a large share of a mix's perceived energy and low end is where most listening happens in mono in practice (see below). **[general engineering fact — DSP/acoustics fundamentals, not a single citable "fact" but universally documented in mixing references, e.g. the phase-cancellation explainers surveyed above]**
- **Practical reasons mono compatibility still matters in 2026**:
  - **Vinyl cutting**: a physically informed constraint — a lathe cutting head has real difficulty tracking a stylus groove when low frequencies are wildly out of phase between channels (large vertical/lateral excursions at bass frequencies risk the cutter head jumping the groove or causing playback mistracking); mastering-for-vinyl guidance near-universally calls for mono (or near-mono) bass below roughly 100–150 Hz.
  - **Club/PA systems**: many club and PA rigs sum to mono for the subwoofer feed (sub content is frequently derived from a mono or heavily-summed signal, sometimes via a mono/LCR crossover), so out-of-phase bass can partially cancel on the system that's supposed to be delivering the most bass.
  - **Phone speakers**: single-driver, effectively mono playback (see below) — no stereo image exists to begin with, so any bass content that depends on stereo phase relationships for its perceived level is straightforwardly at risk of cancelling if summed incorrectly upstream, and more importantly the driver usually cannot reproduce genuine sub-bass at all regardless of phase.
  - **Bluetooth speakers**: many compact/portable Bluetooth speakers are single-driver or use passive radiators fed from a summed/mono-ish signal chain, similar risk profile to phone speakers.

- **Smartphone speaker low-end rolloff — [MEASURED/THIRD-PARTY, but I could not pull a single authoritative number I'd stake a video claim on]**:
  - Independent measurement write-ups I found describe most phone speakers' usable low end rolling off somewhere in the **~250–400 Hz** region, with a search-engine-summarized description citing "small portable electronic devices... cannot generate significant open-air acoustic output at frequencies below approximately 250 Hz" as a general physical limitation of small drivers, and device-specific notes (e.g., one report describing an iPhone model's rolloff beginning near ~400 Hz).
  - I attempted to fetch the primary source that looked most rigorous — Audiokinetic's "Loudness and frequency response on popular smart phones" (https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/loudness-and-frequency-response-on-popular-smart-phones/) — and it returned HTTP 403 (blocked), so **I could not verify its measured numbers directly today**; only search-summarized fragments were available, and those are second-hand paraphrases of that article, not something to put on screen as a hard number.
  - **Recommendation for the video**: state the low-end rolloff as an **order-of-magnitude range (roughly 250–400 Hz, driver- and model-dependent)** rather than a single precise Hz figure, and say plainly that no single number applies to "phone speakers" as a category since it varies by device generation and enclosure. Do not put a bare number like "phones roll off at 300 Hz" on screen as if it were a spec — it isn't one; it's a description of typical small-driver physics.

## 4. Equal-loudness contours (Fletcher-Munson / ISO 226)

- **What the curves show**: the sound pressure level (SPL, dB) needed at each frequency for a pure tone to be perceived as equally loud as a 1 kHz reference tone at a given loudness level (phon). Because ear sensitivity is frequency-dependent — lowest at very low and very high frequencies, most sensitive around 2–5 kHz (ear-canal/ossicle resonance) — the curves are not flat: **at low SPLs (quiet listening), bass and treble both need considerably more SPL than midrange to sound equally loud**; the gap narrows as overall level rises.
- **Why mixes sound bass-light at low volume**: as playback level drops, the ear's non-flat sensitivity curve makes bass (and to a lesser extent treble) fall away perceptually faster than midrange — a mix balanced at loud monitoring levels will sound thin/bass-light when played quietly, and a mix balanced quiet will sound bass-heavy when played loud. This is the direct, practical consequence of the equal-loudness contours flattening out as SPL increases.
- **Current standard — [STANDARD]**: **ISO 226:2023**, "Acoustics — Normal equal-loudness-level contours," approved 2023-03-29, which **replaces ISO 226:2003** (itself a full revision of the earlier Robinson-Dadson/Fletcher-Munson-era standard). Source: Waseda University research summary, "Revision of ISO 226 'Normal Equal-Loudness-Level Contours' from 2003 to 2023 edition," https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast/45/1/45_e23.66/_article — motivated in part by a 2019 revision to the reference hearing-threshold standard (ISO 389-7:2019, which lowered the 20 Hz threshold by 0.4 dB) and updated loudness-perception exponents; **the paper states the 2023 curves differ from the 2003 curves by at most 0.6 dB**, i.e., practically identical for mixing purposes — good on-screen line: "the standard was updated in 2023, but the curves barely moved."
- **Approximate dB difference, 50 Hz vs. 1 kHz, at normal listening level — [ESTIMATE, read off a published ISO 226:2003 chart, not a table lookup]**: reading the official ISO 226:2003 equal-loudness graph (redrawn reference copy: Sengpiel/UdK Berlin, 2005, https://leehite.org/documents/Acoustics226-2003.pdf) at the **40-phon contour** (roughly moderate/quiet listening — by definition, 40 phon = 40 dB SPL at 1 kHz), the curve at 50 Hz sits somewhere in the region of **roughly 60–68 dB SPL**, i.e., **very roughly 20–28 dB more SPL is needed at 50 Hz than at 1 kHz for equal perceived loudness at moderate listening levels**. I want to flag this explicitly: **this is a chart read, not a table value** — I could not extract exact numeric table entries from the ISO 226 PDF (it's a scanned/graphical document), so do not put a single decimal number like "24.3 dB" on screen; say "roughly 20–25 dB" and cite the chart. The gap shrinks substantially at louder listening levels (unverified secondary-source figures suggested something like a ~22 dB gap around 60 phon narrowing to ~12–14 dB around 85 phon — these narrower-level figures came from a non-primary blog and should be treated as **[ESTIMATE, unverified]**, not quoted as precise).

## 5. The loudness war — one paragraph + a measurable illustration

The "loudness war" refers to a decades-long trend, accelerating through the 1990s–2000s, of mastering engineers using increasingly aggressive limiting/compression to make commercial releases louder on average, at the cost of dynamic range (the difference between a track's peak and average level) — a trend that largely reversed in incentive once streaming platforms began normalizing playback loudness (since a louder master no longer sounds louder on a normalized platform, it just loses dynamics for nothing). **Illustration — [MEASURED/THIRD-PARTY, from the community-run Dynamic Range Database]**: Metallica's *Death Magnetic* (2008) measures **DR3** on the Dynamic Range Database (https://dr.loudness-war.info/album/view/104343, DR = crest-factor-based dynamic range score, higher = more dynamic), compared with the same band's *Metallica* ("The Black Album," 1991) reported elsewhere at **DR11** — an eight-point drop in measured dynamic range across 17 years from the same artist, and *Death Magnetic* is one of the most frequently cited textbook examples of loudness-war mastering (its CD master was controversial enough at release that a fan petition asked for a remaster). Flag for the video: DR database scores are a community-measured, crowd-submitted metric (methodology: crest factor averaged over short segments), not an ITU/AES standard — cite it as "independently measured, widely used in mastering discussion," not as an official spec, and if re-measuring for the video, note the DR algorithm version used since the DR database has had multiple scoring-method revisions over its life.
