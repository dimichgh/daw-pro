## 1. The Pythagorean comma

**The setup**: stack twelve justly-tuned perfect fifths (ratio 3:2 each) and compare the result to seven octaves (ratio 2:1 each) of the starting pitch.

- **Exact ratio (exact-by-definition, not measured)**: (3/2)^12 / 2^7 = 531441/524288.
  - 3^12 = 531441, 2^19 = 524288 (twelve fifths = 2^-12 · 3^12 in raw pitch-class terms; dividing out seven octaves = 2^7 leaves 3^12/2^19).
  - Source: [Wikipedia, "Pythagorean comma"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_comma); cross-checked against [Xenharmonic Wiki](https://en.xen.wiki/w/Pythagorean_comma) and [Tonalsoft encyclopedia](http://tonalsoft.com/enc/p/pythagorean-comma.aspx) — all three agree on the ratio.
- **Decimal ratio**: 531441/524288 ≈ 1.013643 (exact-by-definition; I computed this directly from the integers, not quoted from a source, and it matches the Wikipedia figure of ≈1.01364).
- **Cents value (exact-by-definition, derivable from the ratio)**: 1200 · log₂(531441/524288) ≈ **23.46 cents**. I computed this myself (≈23.4577, rounds to 23.46) and it matches the commonly cited figure on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_comma) and [Xenharmonic Wiki](https://en.xen.wiki/w/Pythagorean_comma). Roughly a quarter of an equal-tempered semitone (100 cents).
- **Frequency error you'd actually hear**: the ratio 1.013643 is about a **1.36% frequency excess**. In absolute terms this scales with pitch — e.g. at A440 the equivalent error is roughly 6 Hz, easily audible as fast, clearly-perceptible beating when the two pitches are sounded together or compared in quick succession. This Hz figure is my own arithmetic (440 × 0.013643 ≈ 6.0 Hz), not quoted from a source — flag as **derived, not independently verified against an outside citation**, though the underlying math is exact.
- **What note you land on**: starting from C and stacking 12 justly-tuned perfect fifths (C→G→D→A→E→B→F♯→C♯→G♯→D♯→A♯→E♯→B♯) lands on **B♯**, which is enharmonically "C" on a keyboard but is **23.46 cents sharp** of the C reached by stacking 7 pure octaves from the same starting C. This is the textbook illustration of the comma and is exactly why Pythagorean/just tuning cannot close the circle of fifths. Source: [Wikipedia, "Pythagorean comma"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_comma).
- **Related term for the DAW-relevant story**: if you instead try to tune a full closed circle of 12 fifths on a keyboard using pure 3:2 fifths, the last fifth you're forced to close (traditionally G♯–E♭) comes out flat by this comma — the historical "wolf fifth." (Meantone systems, below, have their own differently-sized wolf; this is the Pythagorean-tuning version of the same phenomenon.)

**On-screen-ready numbers**: 531441/524288, ≈1.01364, 23.46 cents, ~1.36%, ~6 Hz at A440. All exact-by-definition except the 6 Hz figure, which is a direct arithmetic consequence of the exact ratio and depends on which absolute pitch you evaluate it at.

## 2. Why 12-tone equal temperament

- **Tempered vs. just fifth**: the 12-TET fifth is defined as exactly 700 cents (7 semitones out of 1200 cents/octave — exact-by-definition). The just (3:2) fifth is 1200·log₂(1.5) ≈ **701.955 cents**. The tempered fifth is therefore about **1.955 cents narrow** relative to just (commonly rounded to "~2 cents flat"). This is exactly 1/12 of the Pythagorean comma (23.46/12 ≈ 1.955), which is the whole point of equal temperament: spread the comma evenly across all twelve fifths so no single fifth is noticeably worse than another. Source: [Wikipedia, "Perfect fifth"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth); [Tonalsoft, "12-edo"](http://tonalsoft.com/enc/number/12edo.aspx). Exact-by-definition given the 700-cent tempered fifth and the exact just ratio.
- **The interval 12-TET makes worst — the major third**: just major third (5:4) = 1200·log₂(1.25) ≈ **386.31 cents**. Equal-tempered major third = exactly 400 cents (4 semitones). Difference: **400 − 386.31 = 13.69 cents sharp** — nearly a seventh of a semitone, and by far the most-mistuned common consonant interval in 12-TET (much worse than the ~2-cent fifth). Source: [Wikipedia, "Major third"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third). Exact-by-definition given the two ratios.
  - Practical/audible note (useful for the video, not independently sourced beyond general acoustics knowledge): this 13.69-cent sharpness is why 12-TET major triads sound faintly "bright"/tense compared to a barbershop-quartet or just-intonation-tuned triad, and why a cappella singers and string quartets who tune by ear routinely flatten their major thirds relative to a piano.
- **Meantone temperament** (one paragraph): Just intonation's various-sized semitones make a single fixed keyboard tuning unworkable across many keys. Meantone temperaments solve this by tempering the fifths (most famously, quarter-comma meantone flattens each fifth by exactly 1/4 of the syntonic comma) so that four stacked fifths produce a pure 5:4 major third. This gives quarter-comma meantone genuinely pure major thirds in the most-used keys — better consonance than 12-TET for those keys — at the cost of a residual "wolf" interval elsewhere in the circle (traditionally between G♯ and E♭) that is badly out of tune, restricting composers to a limited set of usable keys. Source: [Wikipedia, "Meantone temperament"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meantone_temperament).
- **Well temperament** (one paragraph): Well temperaments (e.g., those associated with Werckmeister, Kirnberger, Vallotti) were developed to eliminate meantone's unusable wolf interval while still letting each key sound subtly different in color — some keys purer/calmer, others more tense — rather than the perfectly uniform sameness of 12-TET. This let composers, notably Bach in the *Well-Tempered Clavier* (a title referencing *wohltemperiert*), write and modulate through all 24 major/minor keys on one fixed keyboard tuning without hitting an unusable wolf interval, while still preserving some of meantone's per-key character. Source: [Wikipedia, "Well temperament"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_temperament).

**On-screen-ready numbers**: tempered fifth 700.00 cents (exact), just fifth 701.96 cents (exact given ratio), difference ≈1.96 cents narrow; tempered major third 400.00 cents (exact), just major third 386.31 cents (exact given ratio), difference ≈13.69 cents sharp.

## 3. A = 440 Hz

### Standardization

- **ISO 16**: the current international standard is **ISO 16:1975** ("Acoustics — Standard tuning frequency (Standard musical pitch)"), which formalized A4 = 440 Hz. It replaced the earlier **ISO Recommendation R 16:1955**, which was technically identical in content. Sources: [ISO 16:1975 catalog entry](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/iso/a9ae0915-e583-4b30-ab2b-552616f7dcd0/iso-16-1975); [Wikipedia, "A440 (pitch standard)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A440_(pitch_standard)). I was not able to read the full paywalled ISO 16:1975 text itself (ISO standards are sold, not freely published) — the 1975 date and "replaces R 16:1955" relationship are corroborated by the catalog listing and Wikipedia, but flag that I have **not personally read the primary ISO document**, only secondary descriptions of it.
- **Path to that standard** (each date/place below sourced to [Wikipedia, "A440 (pitch standard)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A440_(pitch_standard)) unless noted):
  - 1834: physicist Johann Heinrich Scheibler recommended A=440 Hz after building a "tonometer"; approved by the Stuttgart Society of German Natural Scientists and Physicians that year.
  - 1859 (16 Feb): the French government legislated A4 = **435 Hz**, the "diapason normal," after a commission including Berlioz, Rossini, and Meyerbeer responded to singers' complaints about ever-rising pitch. Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A440_(pitch_standard)); corroborated by multiple secondary tuning-history pages.
  - 1885: Austria's government recommended 435 Hz; the Vienna Musical Congress of 1885 voted to broadly endorse the French 435 Hz standard internationally.
  - 1881: the Congresso dei Musicisti Italiani (Milan, 16–21 June) set the Italian diapason at **432 Hz**; an 1884 Italian War Ministry decree mandated A=432 for military/orchestral bands. Italy did not get to nationalize this before the 1885 Vienna congress moved international consensus toward 435 Hz. Source: [Cambridge Core, "Tuning Sounds in Italy, 1750–1885"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nineteenth-century-music-review/article/tuning-sounds-in-italy-17501885/916BC4B2B1E69D460158640DFAA95957) — this is the strongest citation I found for the Italian 432 Hz episode; treat other sources repeating this figure as secondary to this one.
  - 1899: British piano tuners standardized around A=439 Hz.
  - 1926: the American music industry reached an informal 440 Hz consensus.
  - 1936: the American Standards Association officially recommended A=440.
  - 1939 (May): delegates from France, Germany, the Netherlands, Italy, and England met at BBC Broadcasting House, London, and agreed on A=440 as an international reference (not yet a binding international standard).
  - 1955 / 1975: formalized internationally as ISO Recommendation R 16 (1955), then ISO 16 (1975), as above.
- **Present day**: continental European orchestras commonly tune sharper than the nominal standard, frequently in the 440–444 Hz range; this is orchestra/hall practice, not a re-standardization, and I could not find an authoritative single figure for "the" modern European average — flag as **anecdotal/commonly repeated, not independently measured by me**.

### Pitches used before standardization

Before any of this, pitch varied enormously by region, era, and even by instrument type within the same city:

- A surviving tuning fork associated with Handel, c. 1740, is pitched at **A=422.5 Hz**; another Handel-associated fork from 1780 reads **A=409 Hz** — illustrating that even forks tied to one composer/era disagree. Source: search-aggregated from multiple tuning-history pages citing period fork surveys; I could not trace this to a single primary museum catalog entry, so **flag as commonly-repeated but not verified by me against a primary museum record**.
- In Bach's Germany, "Kammerton" (chamber pitch, used for secular ensembles) sat around **A≈415 Hz**, while "Chorton" (choir/organ pitch) sat roughly a whole step higher, around **A≈466 Hz** — organs were often built to a different, higher pitch than the chamber instruments that played with them. Source: general tuning-history secondary sources (Gamut Music, pitchdetector.com); **not independently verified against a primary organ-building record** — treat as broadly correct received wisdom rather than a precise measured figure.
- Overall Baroque-era range: surviving organ pipes and tuning forks put A4 anywhere from roughly **390 Hz to 480 Hz** depending on location, decade, and instrument type. This is a commonly cited range in secondary tuning-history literature; I could not find a single canonical primary-source table listing every value, so treat the *range* as reliable but any specific number within it (other than the ones individually cited above) as unverified by me.

### The "432 Hz" claims — what's true, what's myth

**True, with citations**:
- Verdi did, in correspondence, express a preference for 432 Hz over the French 435 Hz standard on grounds of comfort for singers; Italy did briefly have an official 432 Hz standard for military/orchestral bands (1884 War Ministry decree), traceable to the 1881 Milan musicians' congress. Source: [Cambridge Core, "Tuning Sounds in Italy, 1750–1885"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nineteenth-century-music-review/article/tuning-sounds-in-italy-17501885/916BC4B2B1E69D460158640DFAA95957).
- There is a genuinely different, older rationale that lands near — but not exactly on — 432 Hz: 19th-century "scientific pitch" set middle C at exactly 256 Hz (a clean power of two, convenient for physics/acoustics teaching), which implies A4 ≈ **430.54 Hz**, not 432. The 432 figure that circulates today is often conflated with this "scientific pitch" C=256 lineage even though the numbers don't actually match. Source: [Wikipedia, "Scientific pitch"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_pitch).

**False / unsubstantiated — flag these explicitly for a "myth vs. fact" segment**:
- **"432 Hz was the universal ancient/historical standard before 440."** False — as documented above, historical pitch varied wildly by region and era (roughly 390–480 Hz for A4 depending on time/place); there was no universal pre-440 standard at all, let alone one at 432 Hz specifically. Source: [Lead Stories fact-check](https://leadstories.com/hoax-alert/2022/10/fact-check-tuning-standard-of-a-440-hz-was-not-driven-by-nazi-and-rockefeller-desire-for-discord.html); [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2021/01/27/how-a-centuries-old-debate-about-musical-tuning-turned-into-a-nazi-conspiracy-theory.html).
- **"A=440 was imposed by the Nazis / Goebbels in 1939 to create discord."** False. This claim traces specifically to Leonard Horowitz and Lyndon LaRouche's organization; there is no documented evidence Goebbels or the Nazi government drove the 1939 London pitch conference. The 1939 conference did happen (see above) but was a five-nation musicians'/technical convening, not a Nazi propaganda operation, and A440 had already been gaining ground in the US and UK for over a decade before it. Source: [Lead Stories](https://leadstories.com/hoax-alert/2022/10/fact-check-tuning-standard-of-a-440-hz-was-not-driven-by-nazi-and-rockefeller-desire-for-discord.html); [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2021/01/27/how-a-centuries-old-debate-about-musical-tuning-turned-into-a-nazi-conspiracy-theory.html).
- **"No ancient civilization is documented as tuning to 432 Hz."** Correct as a negative claim per the fact-checks above — there is no archaeological or documentary evidence of ancient tuning to 432 Hz specifically (ancient pitch is not even reliably reconstructible in Hz terms for most cultures/eras). Source: [Lead Stories](https://leadstories.com/hoax-alert/2022/10/fact-check-tuning-standard-of-a-440-hz-was-not-driven-by-nazi-and-rockefeller-desire-for-discord.html).
- **"432 Hz has measurable healing/physiological/'universe frequency' effects."** No peer-reviewed evidence found for this in general web/scholarly searching; claims trace to alternative-medicine and numerology sources (Solfeggio frequencies, "cosmic octave" claims), not acoustics or medical literature. Flag as **unsubstantiated pseudoscience**, good material for a myth-busting segment, but I did not do an exhaustive literature search of medical journals — treat as "no evidence found," not "proven false in the medical literature."
- **The actual audible difference, 440 vs 432 Hz**: 1200·log₂(440/432) ≈ **31.76 cents** — about a third of a semitone. This is my own calculation from the two frequencies (exact-by-definition given the two numbers) and is worth putting on screen: it's a real, small, perceptible-to-trained-ears pitch difference, not nothing — but it's a plain transposition-by-a-third-of-a-semitone, not a mystical frequency with special properties.

## 4. Stretched piano tuning / the Railsback curve

- **Inharmonicity**: a piano string is not an ideal massless string — it has real stiffness (bending resistance), so it behaves partly like a stiff rod. This pushes its overtones (partials) progressively sharp of the ideal integer-multiple harmonic series as partial number increases. The effect grows for shorter, thicker/stiffer strings (bass strings wound with copper, and the shortest treble strings), which is why it's worst at both extremes of the keyboard. Source: [Wikipedia, "Piano tuning"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_tuning); [Wikipedia, "Railsback curve"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railsback_curve).
- **Why pianos are deliberately detuned from equal temperament**: because of inharmonicity, if you tune every note to mathematically exact 12-TET frequencies, octaves stop sounding beatless — the sharp upper partials of the lower note clash with the (correspondingly less sharp, because it's a different string) fundamental/lower partials of the upper note. Tuners instead tune "by ear," matching coincident partials across octaves, which means progressively sharpening treble notes and flattening bass notes relative to theoretical 12-TET. This is called "stretch tuning," and the resulting deviation-from-equal-temperament curve is the **Railsback curve**, named for O.L. Railsback, a US physics teacher/researcher who measured it empirically across multiple pianos and published it in *The Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* in **1938**. Source: [Wikipedia, "Railsback curve"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railsback_curve).
- **Typical deviation magnitude at the extremes — FLAG: sources disagree, no single canonical figure**. I found real spread across secondary sources describing the same curve:
  - One description: roughly **+15 to +20 cents sharp** at the top octave, similar magnitude flat at the very bottom.
  - Another (same aggregator page, inconsistent within itself): **+20 to +30 cents** at the highest treble notes (near C8/key 88) and **−10 to −20 cents** at the lowest bass notes (near A0/key 1).
  - Source for both, inconsistent with each other: [FrequencyDetector.com, "Piano Stretch Tuning Calculator"](https://frequencydetector.com/piano-stretch-tuning-calculator/).
  - What is consistent and well-supported across all sources: the curve is (a) an S-shape, (b) near-zero deviation in the middle octaves around A4, (c) it is **empirically derived from real tuners' work, not a fixed formula** — it varies by piano (scale design, string length, string gauge), so there is no single "correct" number, only a typical range. The [Wikipedia article on Railsback curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railsback_curve) itself explicitly declines to give one canonical number for this reason.
  - **Recommendation for the video**: do not put one precise cents figure on screen for "typical" deviation at the extremes — say "on the order of 15–30 cents" and cite that this is instrument-dependent, or better, measure a real piano's inharmonicity live for the segment (this is directly checkable in DAW Pro / any tuner app against a real acoustic piano) rather than quoting a textbook number, which is exactly the kind of thing this channel should demonstrate rather than assert.

**On-screen-ready numbers**: Railsback curve published 1938 (Journal of the Acoustical Society of America) — this date is solid. Deviation magnitude — do NOT present as a single precise number; present as a range (~15–30 cents at extremes) with the caveat that it's empirical and piano-specific, or measure it live.

## Actionable takeaways

1. **Script-ready exact figures** (safe to put on screen as-is, all independently recomputed by me from first principles, not just quoted):
   - Pythagorean comma: 531441/524288 ≈ 1.01364, 23.46 cents, ~1.36% frequency error.
   - 12-TET fifth vs. just fifth: 700.00 vs 701.96 cents, ~1.96 cents narrow (exactly 1/12 of the Pythagorean comma — good visual for the episode: "the comma, divided evenly across 12 fifths").
   - 12-TET major third vs. just major third: 400.00 vs 386.31 cents, 13.69 cents sharp — this is the strongest "12-TET's dirty secret" segment; the third is ~7x worse than the fifth.
   - A440 vs. A432: 31.76 cents (~1/3 semitone) — use this to visually deflate the 432 Hz mystique: it's a small, ordinary, measurable transposition, not a special frequency.

2. **Strong myth-busting segment, well-sourced**: the "440 Hz = Nazi conspiracy" and "432 Hz = ancient universal/healing frequency" claims are both traceable to specific 20th-century fringe sources (LaRouche/Horowitz) and are contradicted by the actual, much messier history (pitch ranged ~390–480 Hz across the Baroque era alone, with genuine historical A=432 use in 1880s Italy for entirely mundane vocal-comfort reasons, not mysticism). This is exactly the kind of segment where "the real history is more interesting than the myth" — recommend building an episode around it.

3. **Gap flagged, do not paper over it**: the Railsback curve has no single canonical "cents at the extremes" number — sources I found range from 15 to 30 cents depending on which secondary aggregator you read, and Wikipedia itself declines to give one fixed figure because it's empirical and piano-specific. For a channel whose whole brand is "the number is on screen," the correct move is either (a) explicitly show the range with the caveat, or (b) — stronger for this channel's brand — measure a real piano's actual Railsback deviation live using DAW Pro's own tuner/pitch-detection path and put *that* measured number on screen instead of a textbook figure. This doubles as a good dogfooding case for DAW Pro's pitch-detection/tuning tooling if it has (or should grow) a note-accurate frequency readout.

4. **ISO 16:1975 primary text gap**: I could not read the actual ISO 16:1975 standard (it's a paid ISO publication); the 1975 date and its relationship to the 1955 R 16 recommendation are corroborated by secondary sources (catalog listing + Wikipedia) but not verified against the primary document itself. If this fact is going to be stated with high confidence on screen ("ISO 16:1975, and here is what it says"), consider purchasing/accessing the actual ISO document rather than relying on secondary description.

5. **Suggested episode structure** (not required, offered as a byproduct of doing this research): Pythagorean comma (why perfect fifths and octaves are mathematically incompatible) → equal temperament as the pragmatic fix, and its real cost (the sharp major third) → meantone/well temperament as the historical intermediate steps → A440 as an arbitrary-but-standardized convention with a genuinely messy, non-mystical history → Railsback stretch tuning as a case where "correct" tuning deliberately deviates from the "exact" math for a good physical reason, measured live rather than quoted. This progression moves from exact math → convention → physical reality, which mirrors the "measured, not assumed" brand well.
