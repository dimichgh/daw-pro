## 1. Bach, Prelude in C major, BWV 846 (Well-Tempered Clavier, Book 1)

### Length and structure
- **[EXACT]** The prelude, as Bach wrote it (autograph manuscript), is **35 measures long**. Source: [Wikipedia, "Prelude and Fugue in C major, BWV 846"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelude_and_Fugue_in_C_major,_BWV_846); corroborated by the Henle blog (below).
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED]** Structural division (corroborated by [tonic-chord.com analysis](https://tonic-chord.com/bach-prelude-and-fugue-no-1-in-c-major-bwv-846-analysis/) and schema-theory description found via search, presumably drawing on Gjerdingen-style galant-schema analysis):
  - **Period I, bars 1–11**: bars 1–4 establish C major (tonic); bars 5–9 move toward the dominant (G major) — bar 5's "6-chord on C" announces the move, an extended **Prinner** schema carries it — ending with a perfect cadence in G at bar 11.
  - **Period II, bars 11–19**: begins in the dominant (G), a **Fonte** schema in bars 12–15 (a descending sequential pattern, roughly Dm → C in function) walks it back toward the tonic, cadencing in C around bar 17–19. Bars 13 and 15 each contain a diminished-seventh chord resolving onto an inversion of the supertonic and the tonic respectively.
  - **Period III, bars 20–35 (coda)**: bars 22–23 contain the famous chromatic bass motion **F♯ → A♭** (a diminished third in the bass, hinting at C minor) — this is the passage the disputed "Schwencke measure" tries to "fix" (see below). A dominant pedal begins at bar 24 and underpins the close.

### Figuration pattern
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED]** The prelude is, harmonically, a chorale-like chord progression (one harmony per bar, almost without exception) disguised as continuous motion by a single repeating broken-chord (arpeggio) figure: a short melodic cell that is **stated, then repeated once more within the same bar** ("a constant reiteration of the characteristic figure...which is repeated twice in every bar until the close, when it is expanded for variety into a freer and wider-ranging arpeggio" — paraphrased from 19th-century analysis quoted at [tonic-chord.com](https://tonic-chord.com/bach-prelude-and-fugue-no-1-in-c-major-bwv-846-analysis/)). Each bar = 8 sixteenth notes in 4/4, i.e., the figure is a 4-note (or similar short) cell played twice.
- **[UNVERIFIED / ANALYST INFERENCE — DO NOT PUT ON SCREEN WITHOUT CHECKING THE SCORE]** I was not able to fetch a literal note-by-note (pitch-by-pitch) or bar-by-bar roman-numeral transcription from a live source in this session (Hooktheory and ResearchGate both blocked automated extraction; a PDF score analysis at edition-gorz.de was unreadable as fetched). What I recall from training data about the exact pitch content of the figure (a rising broken-chord cell such as C4-E4-G4-C5-E4-G4-C5-E5 in bar 1) is **plausible but not independently re-verified against the score this session** — before keying exact pitches into the DAW, cross-check bar-by-bar against the public-domain IMSLP score (link below), specifically the Bach-Gesellschaft (Kroll, 1866) or Neue Bach-Ausgabe (Dürr, 1989) editions, both marked public domain.
  - Roman-numeral skeleton that IS corroborated by two independent sources (Hooktheory's summary and the structural analysis above): **bars 1–4: I – ii7 – V7 – I** (in C); **bars 5–7: ii – V7 – I** (tonicizing G, the dominant). This much I'm confident stating on screen. Bars 8–16 roman numerals beyond this were not independently confirmed this session — verify against score before publishing.

### The Schwencke measure controversy
- **[EXACT]** Christian Friedrich Gottlieb Schwencke (1767–1822), a Hamburg music director, added an extra measure between **bars 22 and 23** in his manuscript copy of WTC I, dated **1783**. It's built on a bass note G and regularizes what he considered an "abrupt and audacious" chromatic move (Bach's original F♯→A♭ bass leap). Source: [Henle Blog, "On the lookout for the lost measure"](https://blog.henle.de/en/2012/04/16/on-the-lookout-for-the-lost-measure-bach%E2%80%99s-c-major-prelude-from-the-well-tempered-clavier-i/).
- **[EXACT]** The interpolated measure subsequently entered print via **Simrock's 1801/1802 edition** and **Carl Czerny's 1837 Peters edition**, and from there into 19th-century performance tradition — including the version **Gounod used as the harmonic bed for "Ave Maria."** Modern scholarly/urtext editions omit it. Source: same Henle blog; corroborated by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelude_and_Fugue_in_C_major,_BWV_846).
- **[EXACT]** **Franz Kroll** first challenged the extra measure's authenticity in his 1862/1866 Bach-Gesellschaft edition; it is absent from **Heinrich Nikolaus Gerber's 1725 manuscript copy** (a copy made directly from Bach's circle, decades before Schwencke). Bach's autograph — 35 measures, no interpolation — is the controlling source. Musicologist Hermann Keller's verdict, quoted in the Henle blog: Schwencke "was a sophisticated and well-informed musician who was probably not thinking of improving Bach" — i.e., he likely believed (wrongly) that a measure had been lost in transmission, rather than deliberately "fixing" Bach.
- **Verdict for the video: this is settled, not disputed** — the autograph is unambiguous, and virtually all modern editions (Bärenreiter NBA, Henle, etc.) print 35 bars. The controversy is historical/textual-critical interest (how a copyist's "correction" became embedded in 19th-century performance tradition for decades), not an open musicological question today.

### Public domain: composition vs. edition vs. MIDI file
- **[EXACT]** The **composition** is unambiguously public domain worldwide: Bach died in 1750, more than 250 years before any copyright term (life+70, the longest common term) could apply. Source: [IMSLP work page](https://imslp.org/wiki/Prelude_and_Fugue_in_C_major,_BWV_846_(Bach,_Johann_Sebastian)).
- **[EXACT]** But **specific typeset editions can carry their own, separate copyright** — the editor's engraving, fingering, ornament realization, and (for urtext editions) critical-apparatus choices are original creative/scholarly work distinct from Bach's notes. IMSLP's own listing for this piece shows the split explicitly: the 1866 Bach-Gesellschaft (Kroll) and 1893 Czerny/Schirmer editions are flagged plain "Public Domain," while the 1912 Schweitzer (Universal Edition), 1957 Georgii (Schott), and — notably — the **1989 Neue Bach-Ausgabe urtext (Dürr, Bärenreiter)** and **1993 Könemann urtext (Zászkaliczky)** are flagged **"Public Domain [US] / Non-PD EU"** or "see notes on copyright status for urtext editions." That means: in the US the composition being pre-1928/pre-life+70 makes even these editions free of the underlying-work copyright, but in the EU, a modern scholarly edition's own editorial content can still be protected. Source: [IMSLP work page](https://imslp.org/wiki/Prelude_and_Fugue_in_C_major,_BWV_846_(Bach,_Johann_Sebastian)) (fetched 2026-09-03).
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED, general principle]** A **MIDI file transcription** of the piece is a *derivative performance-data artifact*, not the composition itself — if someone hand-entered fingering, articulation, or a specific arrangement/voicing choice into that MIDI file, that arrangement can itself be a copyrightable derivative work (this is the same logic as the edition case, just applied to a MIDI file instead of engraved notation). The safest practice for the video — and what I'd recommend on screen — is: state that the *composition* BWV 846 is public domain (cite Bach's 1750 death date), but that **any specific edition or MIDI file downloaded from the internet may carry its own transcriber/editor copyright**, and the channel's own re-entry (typing the notes in by hand from the public-domain autograph/urtext) sidesteps that entirely. I could not find a single authoritative case-law citation on MIDI-file-specific copyright in the time available this session — flag this paragraph's legal framing as **[WELL-ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLE, not case-law-verified]**.

## 2. Beethoven's metronome markings — present as an OPEN QUESTION

- **[EXACT]** Scope of the problem: of Beethoven's **135 metronome markings**, **66** have historically been regarded by performers/scholars as "absurdly fast and thus possibly wrong." Source cited via [Forsén et al., 2013 mathematical analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259214087_Waa_Something_Wrong_with_Beethovens_Metronome), summarized in [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/was-beethovens-metronome-wrong-9140958/).
- **[EXACT, named examples]**
  - **Symphony No. 5, first movement (Allegro con brio)**: marked **half note = 108**. Source: [popularbeethoven.com, "The tempo in Beethoven's Fifth Symphony"](https://www.popularbeethoven.com/the-tempo-in-beethovens-fifth-symphony/), corroborated by search summary of Kenneth Woods' blog.
  - **Symphony No. 3 "Eroica," finale (Allegro molto)**: marked **half note = 76** — commonly cited alongside No. 5's marking as evidence markings vary meaningfully movement to movement even under the same tempo-word ("allegro con brio"/"allegro molto"), which is one argument AGAINST the "the metronome itself was simply broken at one fixed rate" theory. Same source as above.
  - **[MEASURED, historical performance data]** Gunther Schuller, in a 1998 survey, averaged **66 recordings** of the Fifth Symphony's finale and found a mean tempo of **~92 bpm** — well under Beethoven's marked 108, illustrating how far performance practice has drifted from the marking. Conductor Leopold Stokowski went as slow as **~40 bpm** at one extreme (unit not specified in source — treat this figure with caution, it's an extreme outlier cited anecdotally). Source: search-result summary of Kenneth Woods blog content — **[secondary citation, verify against Woods' original post before using the Stokowski figure specifically]**.
  - **Symphony No. 9, finale, "Turkish march" (Allegro assai vivace alla marcia)**: this is the single most contested marking in the literature. It's recorded in Beethoven's conversation books (via his nephew Karl) as **dotted-quarter = 84**, but Karl didn't write down the note value himself — it was added later — and at dotted-quarter=84 the march is very slow. **Hermann Beck argued in the 1950s** (and it's now widely, though not universally, accepted) that Beethoven meant **dotted-half = 84**, three times faster, which fits better with: (a) other Beethoven markings for similar march-style movements, and (b) the internal tempo logic of the finale, where the "Ode to Joy" theme is marked at 80 bpm on first appearance, 84 bpm at the double fugue, and 88 bpm ("Prestissimo") at the close — a marking that only makes narrative/tempo sense if the march tempo is also read in the faster unit. Source: [Oxford Academic, "The metronome marks for Beethoven's Ninth Symphony in context," Early Music, 2021](https://academic.oup.com/em/article/49/1/129/6276767), corroborated by search summary. **[DISPUTED — this dotted-quarter vs dotted-half reading is still argued in the scholarly literature, not settled.]**

### Three competing explanations for "too fast" markings — present all three, none is proven
1. **Broken/damaged metronome hypothesis.** Sture Forsén and colleagues (mathematicians/music historians), in a **2013** paper, modeled Beethoven's double-pendulum Maelzel metronome mathematically and showed a mechanically damaged unit — "perhaps accidentally during one of his well-known violent temper tantrums," per the paper's framing — could produce readings consistent with his unusually fast markings. Beethoven's own letters complain of the device's "uneven pulse" and record repeated repairs. His actual metronome survives (located by musicologist Peter Stadlen) but is missing its weight, so it can't be tested directly. Source: [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/was-beethovens-metronome-wrong-9140958/); [ResearchGate paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259214087_Waa_Something_Wrong_with_Beethovens_Metronome). **[DISPUTED]**
2. **Misreading-the-scale hypothesis.** Almudena Martín-Castro and Iñaki Ucar, **"Conductors' tempo choices shed light over Beethoven's metronome," PLOS ONE, 2020** ([full paper](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0243616)) — a large-scale study of **36 complete symphonic recordings across 9 symphonies (Beethoven's Ninth excluded), 36 different conductors, 1,188 audio files, over 169 hours of music**, with automated tempo extraction validated by hand on curated samples. They found conductors performed on average **~12–13 bpm slower** than Beethoven's markings, and that **44 of 63 markings** could be explained if Beethoven (or an assistant) misread the moving weight against the wrong side of the scale marker on the physical device — an easy, well-documented type of error on the era's Maelzel metronomes. The paper explicitly **rejects the broken-metronome hypothesis** as the primary explanation, on the strength of this consistent ~12 bpm offset. **[MEASURED — this is the best-evidenced single study found this session, but it is one study, not consensus.]**
3. **Different performance conventions / notational-unit confusion.** Some scholars (e.g., Max Rudolf, and the Ninth Symphony march-tempo debate above) argue some "impossible" markings reflect either (a) confusion over which note value the marking refers to (quarter vs. dotted-quarter vs. half, as in the Ninth's march), or (b) that Beethoven's markings encode *character/energy*, meant to be tempered by the performer's judgment, rather than a literal, unbending tempo — a view historically-informed-performance conductors like Benjamin Zander dispute (Zander advocates following the markings closely but "leavened with flexibility and rubato"). Source: [The Strad, Benjamin Zander on Beethoven's Ninth](https://www.thestrad.com/playing-hub/beyond-the-metronome-benjamin-zander-on-beethovens-ninth-symphony/16118.article); [lvbeethoven.com, "The Ongoing Debate"](https://lvbeethoven.com/the-ongoing-debate-revisiting-beethovens-metronome-marks/). **[WELL-ESTABLISHED that this view exists and is argued by named practitioners; not a "finding," an interpretive stance.]**

**Script framing recommendation:** state clearly that this is unresolved — three explanations (mechanical fault, reading error, notational/interpretive convention) all have real evidence and named advocates, the best single data-driven study (Martín-Castro & Ucar 2020) leans toward reading error but explicitly used a 36-recording sample that *excluded* the Ninth, the piece with the single most contested marking. Do not present any one explanation as "the answer."

## 3. The Amen break

### Source recording
- **[EXACT]** Song: **"Amen, Brother"** by **The Winstons**, a Washington, D.C. soul group; released **1969** as the B-side to "Color Him Father." Drummer: **Gregory Coleman** (bandleader/writer of record: Richard Lewis Spencer). Source: [Wikipedia, "Amen break"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break).
- **[EXACT]** The break occurs at approximately **1:26** into the track, is a **4-bar drum break**, and lasts **~7 seconds** (some sources round to "6 seconds" — treat 7 as the better-sourced figure). Source: Wikipedia, corroborated across multiple search summaries.
- **[EXACT, with a caveat]** Tempo: commonly cited as **~136 bpm** (Wikipedia's figure); some technical/production sources round to **137–140 bpm**. Present on screen as **"approximately 136–140 bpm"** rather than a single precise number — this is a tapped/estimated tempo on a human performance with natural fluctuation, not a metronome-quantized original. Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break); [MusicRadar tutorial](https://www.musicradar.com/tuition/tech/how-to-program-an-amen-style-break-637374) (cites 137).

### Why it matters in jungle/drum & bass
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED]** From the late 1980s (via hip-hop sampling first, e.g. on records that popularized breakbeat sampling) into early-1990s UK hardcore/breakbeat and then jungle/drum & bass, producers pitched up, chopped, re-sequenced and time-stretched this break as the rhythmic backbone of the genre. Its appeal (per multiple sources): the recording's inherent "crunch"/lo-fi texture, and the way Coleman's own syncopation in bars 3–4 (see pattern below) gave producers a ready-made, humanly-swung template they could chop into new patterns rather than programming from scratch. It is frequently cited as "the most sampled drum break/loop in music history," appearing in an estimated thousands of tracks. Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break); [monosonic.studio](https://www.monosonic.studio/post/amen-break).

### The drum pattern, for hand-programming as MIDI (not sample use)
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED, structural description — exact 16th-grid positions below are a reasonable programming approximation, NOT a claimed sample-exact transcription]**
  - **Bars 1–2**: a straight funk drum pattern, repeated identically between the two bars — ride cymbal plays continuous eighth notes throughout; kick drum has a "double-hit" figure around beat 4 (commonly programmed as a 16th-note kick on the "and" of beat 1 and another around beat 4/4-and); snare backbeat lands squarely on **beats 2 and 4**, with the snare closing out bar 2 played noticeably quieter (a ghost/end-of-phrase snare).
  - **Bar 3**: same basic skeleton as bars 1–2, but the **snare hit that would normally land on beat 4 is delayed** — pulled to a syncopated off-beat position — deliberately breaking the listener's expectation of another straight repetition.
  - **Bar 4**: the most-sampled/most-distinctive bar — **beat 1 is left empty** (no kick on the downbeat), followed by a syncopated flurry of snare hits, a crash cymbal landing between beats 3 and 4 (commonly programmed on the "and" of beat 3), and the phrase resolves back into the top of the loop.
  - Sources: search-summarized descriptions from [drummy.io](https://drummy.io/rhythms/amen-break), [MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/tuition/tech/how-to-program-an-amen-style-break-637374), and Wikipedia's summary of the pattern.
- **[IMPORTANT CAVEAT — flag on screen]** Ethan Hein's technical breakdown explicitly warns that a literal 16th-note-grid MIDI transcription **cannot fully capture the original**: "hardly any of [Coleman's hits] are exactly where they are 'supposed' to be" — the groove's feel depends on real human microtiming (hits landing slightly ahead of or behind the grid), which is lost in a quantized MIDI re-programming. Source: [Ethan Hein, "Building the Amen Break," 2023](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2023/building-the-amen-break/). For the video: it's honest and correct to say "here's the skeleton, program it, then deliberately push a few hits off the grid if you want the original's swagger" rather than presenting a MIDI grid as a note-perfect transcription.

### Copyright situation — summarize accurately
- **[EXACT]** **Neither** bandleader Richard Lewis Spencer **nor** drummer Gregory Coleman **ever received royalties** for the break's decades of sampling. Spencer only learned of its widespread use in 1996, when a record-label executive requested the master tape — by which point any copyright-infringement claim was past the statute of limitations he could act on. Coleman died homeless in 2006, reportedly without knowing the scale of his influence. In 2015, a UK fan-led **GoFundMe campaign raised roughly £18,000 (~$26,000/$32,000)** for Spencer as a goodwill gesture — not a legal settlement, not damages, a gift. Source: [Wikipedia, "Amen break"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break).
- **[UNVERIFIED — flag]** "Amen, Brother" being inducted into the U.S. National Recording Registry was mentioned in one fetched summary with a year that reads as "2026" in that source; the National Recording Registry's most recent published class at my training cutoff was 2024/2025-era, so **do not state this induction/year on screen without re-checking the Library of Congress's own registry list directly** before publishing — this smells like it could be a stale/garbled date from the fetch.
- **Bottom line for the video, stated correctly**: the underlying sound recording ("Amen, Brother," 1969) is **still under copyright** (sound recordings from 1969 are protected in the US into the 2060s under current law) — it is **not** public domain, and its sampling history is a story of *uncompensated, largely unlicensed use that predates modern sample-clearance norms and was never legally tested*, not a story of an open/PD recording. This is exactly why the plan to **program the pattern as original MIDI rather than sample the recording** is the legally clean approach, and worth saying explicitly on screen as the reason.

## 4. The Shepard tone

- **[EXACT]** First described by **Roger N. Shepard**, cognitive scientist at Bell Labs, in **"Circularity in Judgments of Relative Pitch," Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, vol. 36, issue 12, pp. 2346–2353, December 1964.** Source: [Wikipedia, "Shepard tone"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepard_tone).
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED, construction recipe]**
  - **Components**: a bank of **sine waves spaced exactly one octave apart** (e.g., if one component sits at 220 Hz, others sit at 110, 55, 27.5 Hz below it and 440, 880, 1760 Hz above it, etc.) spanning the audible range. Shepard's original implementation used roughly **10 components** spanning the audible spectrum in his synthesis (this specific count is commonly cited in secondary/pedagogical sources but I could not re-confirm "10" against the original 1964 paper's text directly this session — **treat the exact original component count as [UNVERIFIED]; for building your own generator, the important thing is "enough octave-spaced sines to cover ~20 Hz–20 kHz," which is typically 8–10 components**).
  - **Amplitude envelope**: each component's amplitude is set by a **fixed bell-shaped window over log-frequency** — Wikipedia's sourced description of Shepard's original is a **raised-cosine function of the component's distance in semitones from a fixed peak frequency**; other write-ups describe it (functionally equivalently, for this purpose) as Gaussian. Shepard himself is quoted as saying "almost any smooth distribution that tapers off to subthreshold levels at low and high frequencies would have done as well as the cosine curve actually employed" — i.e., the *specific* curve shape (cosine vs. Gaussian vs. anything else smooth and symmetric-in-log-frequency, tapering to near-silence at both ends) is not load-bearing; what matters is: loud in the middle of the audible range, fading to inaudible at the top and bottom. Source: [Wikipedia, "Shepard tone"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepard_tone).
  - **How the illusion works / how to build it**: play all components simultaneously and shift ALL of them upward (or downward) in pitch together, in lock-step, continuously. As a component approaches the top of the envelope it fades out (its amplitude falls per the envelope) at the same time a new component fades in an octave below at the bottom of the envelope — because they're all spaced by exactly one octave, the fading-out top component and the fading-in bottom component are, perceptually, "the same pitch class," so the ear hears continuous upward (or downward) motion with no perceptible reset. Practical implementation: pick a base set of frequencies at octave intervals; continuously multiply all of them by the same slowly-increasing ratio (or, in a discrete/stepped version — the classic "Shepard scale" — increment all of them by one semitone at a time, looping each component back down an octave once it exceeds the top of your defined range).
  - **How to make the loop seamless**: because the frequency shift is exactly logarithmic and the components are exactly one octave apart, after the total pitch shift accumulates to **exactly one octave (12 semitones)**, the whole bank of frequencies is — by construction — identical to where it started (each component has effectively swapped places with its neighbor). So the natural, mathematically seamless loop period is **the time it takes to glide through one octave** at whatever ascent/descent rate you choose; loop the audio buffer at that exact point and the transition is inaudible.
  - **Verifying with a spectrum analyzer**: on a spectrogram, a working Shepard tone shows a set of **horizontal-band harmonics spaced exactly 1200 cents (one octave) apart in log-frequency**, sliding uniformly upward (or downward) together, with each band's brightness/level rising as it crosses the middle of the visible range and dimming as it approaches the top/bottom edges — that "rise, cross the middle, dim as it approaches the edges" envelope on each band is the visual signature to check for; if bands appear/disappear abruptly (a hard cut rather than a fade) the illusion will be audible as a "seam."
- **[EXACT, related]** The continuous-glide (as opposed to discrete-semitone-step) version is called the **Shepard–Risset glissando**, developed by composer **Jean-Claude Risset**. Source: [Wikipedia, "Shepard tone"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepard_tone).

## 5. The tritone / "diabolus in musica" — what's true vs. myth

**This is the section built around a correction — lead with the myth, then correct it, cite named scholars.**

- **THE MYTH (commonly repeated, false as literally stated):** that the medieval Catholic Church formally banned the tritone, and that composers who used it risked excommunication, torture, or were believed to be "summoning the Devil."
- **[WELL-ESTABLISHED — no evidence for the myth]** No historical records document any composer being punished, tried, tortured, imprisoned, or excommunicated specifically for using a tritone. Multiple named musicologists make this point explicitly:
  - **Dr. Sonja Maurer-Dass** (Canadian musicologist specializing in late medieval English music), writing for Medievalists.net: the tritone "occurred very rarely in medieval compositions, and it was generally discouraged," but this was **practical, not theological/supernatural** — the interval sounds dissonant and, critically, **is genuinely hard for an unaccompanied singer to hit accurately in tune**, which mattered enormously in a cappella liturgical singing. Source: [Medievalists.net, "Diabolus in Musica," Dec. 2025](https://www.medievalists.net/2025/12/medieval-church-tritone-devil/) (**note the publication date on this specific article is 2025-12, i.e. recent — flag as fetched 2026-09-03**).
  - **Gerald Moshell**, Professor of Music, Trinity College, quoted via WQXR/Open Culture: the tritone's unsettling quality is straightforward music psychology (it's tonally ambiguous/unresolved, "you don't know where it'll go, but it can't stop where it is"), not a supernatural property. Source: [Open Culture, "The Medieval Ban Against the Devil's Tritone: Debunking a Great Myth"](https://www.openculture.com/2021/10/the-medieval-ban-against-the-devils-tritone-debunking-a-great-myth-in-music-theory.html).
  - **Counter-evidence that the interval wasn't actually forbidden in practice**: 13th-century composer **Pérotin's** piece *"Dum Sigillum Summi Patris"* contains multiple tritones — if there were a hard prohibition, this major sacred-repertoire work would be a glaring violation with, again, zero record of any consequence. Source: same Open Culture / Medievalists.net summaries.
- **[EXACT]** What actually existed was a **pedagogical mnemonic for singers**, not a church decree: **"Mi contra fa est diabolus in musica"** ("mi against fa is the devil in music") — a rule of thumb in solmization (the Guidonian hexachord system) telling singers to avoid the specific melodic/harmonic clash between scale-degrees "fa" and "mi" a tritone apart, because it was hard to tune. Both sources agree the "diabolus in musica" phrase/association is attested **only from around the 18th century onward** — i.e., it postdates the medieval period it's commonly (and wrongly) attached to by several hundred years. Sources: [Medievalists.net](https://www.medievalists.net/2025/12/medieval-church-tritone-devil/); [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2021/10/the-medieval-ban-against-the-devils-tritone-debunking-a-great-myth-in-music-theory.html).
- **What IS true and worth keeping in the script**: the tritone was **discouraged** in medieval melodic writing and in the fa/mi solmization rule — that part is real and documented. What's false is everything supernatural/institutional layered on top of it later (Church prohibition, excommunication, "devil-summoning" belief) — that layer is a much later (at least 18th-century, arguably more a 19th/20th-century popular-writing embellishment) retrofit onto a genuine but mundane pedagogical rule of thumb.
- **[UNVERIFIED — could not confirm this session]** I could not independently verify a specific primary medieval treatise text (e.g., an exact Guido of Arezzo quotation) for the fa/mi rule in this session — Wikipedia's "Diabolus in musica" page returned only as a disambiguation stub when fetched, with no body content. Before scripting a direct quotation attributed to a named medieval theorist, verify it against a dedicated musicological source (e.g., a JSTOR article or a peer-reviewed history of music theory) rather than the popular-press pieces cited above.

## Actionable takeaways

This is a research doc for a YouTube lesson series, not a DAW feature request — but a few things surfaced that are relevant to the DAW Pro product and worth routing:

1. **MIDI-authored public-domain repertoire as demo/onboarding content.** The Bach BWV 846 case study (hand-entering a public-domain composition from a public-domain edition to sidestep any MIDI-file/edition copyright) is a clean, low-risk pattern DAW Pro could reuse for its own onboarding demo projects or MCP-agent "compose in the style of X" examples — cite the composition's PD status and the *specific* PD edition used (e.g., NBA/Dürr 1989, PD in the US) rather than "public domain" as a blanket claim. Consider a `docs/` note (or an MCP tool description) reminding agents that "public domain composition" and "this MIDI/score file I'm reading" are not the same copyright question.
2. **Shepard-tone generator as a shippable synth/FX preset.** The construction recipe above (octave-spaced sine bank, bell-shaped log-frequency envelope, one-octave-loop-period) is fully specified and buildable as a DAWEngine test signal or a built-in "riser/Shepard tone" generator — cheap, novel, and demo-friendly for a mixing/mastering or sound-design lesson episode. If there's appetite, this could become a small `research-analyst` → `audio-dsp-engineer` handoff: a real-time-safe Shepard-tone oscillator (fixed component count, precomputed log-envelope table, no allocation on the render thread) would be a self-contained, well-specified DSP task.
3. **Amen break as a "swing/humanize" case study for the sequencer.** Ethan Hein's point — that quantized MIDI can't reproduce Coleman's microtiming, and that's precisely what gives the break its feel — is a good hook for a DAW Pro episode on humanize/groove-template features (if/when DAW Pro has a swing or groove-quantize tool). Worth checking whether `DAWCore`/`DAWEngine` already exposes a per-note micro-timing offset control that could demo this; if not, it's a small, well-motivated feature idea for a future roadmap item, not urgent.
4. **Fact-checking discipline for future research docs on this channel**: several sources this session either failed to fetch cleanly (Hooktheory's rendered chord diagrams, a corrupted PDF, a stub Wikipedia disambiguation page) or produced numbers I could not cross-verify (the "10 components" Shepard-tone claim, the Amen-break National Recording Registry induction year). All are flagged inline above as UNVERIFIED rather than stated as fact. Recommend the video scripts explicitly carry these caveats rather than smoothing them over — it's consistent with the channel's stated brand ("every claim is measured") and the two flagged items are exactly the kind of thing a sharp-eyed commenter would catch if stated wrong.
